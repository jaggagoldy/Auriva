01 · Market & Method

The ground Auriva stands on

Six archetypes are modeled as they operate in India today — composites of tier-1 metro, tier-2 city and semi-urban reality. Where numbers vary by geography they are given as ranges. Nothing here proposes software; every claim is about the business.

Structural facts that shape every org in this document
FactBusiness consequence
Private sector delivers most care — roughly 70% of outpatient and ~60% of inpatient episodesAuriva's customers are mostly private, revenue-anxious, owner-operated businesses — not budget-funded institutions. Software must pay for itself in visible money or time.
Out-of-pocket still ~45–50% of spend, but falling as PM-JAY (~55 crore covered) and retail insurance growCash-price sensitivity coexists with rising payer paperwork. Every org now runs two parallel money machines: cash counters and claims desks.
Extreme fragmentation — the median provider is a 1-doctor clinic or a <30-bed nursing home, not a corporate hospitalThe platform must be viable at 1 doctor + 1 receptionist, yet coherent at 300 beds. One-size enterprise HIMS has repeatedly failed this market.
Clinical staff scarcity — chronic nursing and specialist shortages, heavy attrition to Gulf/UK, pathologist & radiologist scarcity outside metrosWorkflows are built around scarce people's time (visiting consultants, sessional anesthetists, part-time pathologists), not around departments.
ABDM rails are real now — ~90 crore ABHA accounts (2026), facility & professional registries, consent-managed record exchange; NHCX live since 2025 for standardized claimsFor the first time, longitudinal records and standardized claims are national infrastructure, not vendor lock-in. Orgs that plug in early gain payer and patient advantages.
Regulatory patchwork — Clinical Establishments Act (state-by-state), NABH/NABL (voluntary but payer-linked), DPDP Act, Telemedicine Guidelines 2020, Schedule H/H1/X, PC-PNDT, AERB, Bio-Medical Waste RulesCompliance is a standing operational workload — registers, audits, sign-offs — mostly done on paper today, and a real fear for owners.
Method

Each of the following six sections models one organization archetype across the nine mandated lenses: 1 Business model · 2 Daily operations · 3 Staff structure · 4 Patient journey · 5 Internal workflows · 6 Pain points · 7 Software used today · 8 Biggest opportunities · 9 Future trends — closing with a Challenge block that questions a workflow the industry treats as normal. 02 · Organization Archetype

Independent Clinic

The single-doctor (or husband-wife duo) OPD clinic — the most common healthcare business in India by an enormous margin, and the front door for most episodes of care. General practice, pediatrics, gynecology, dermatology, dental.

Revenue engineConsultation fees, ₹200–1,500/visit
Capacity unitDoctor-hours — the hard ceiling
Typical scale1–2 doctors · 1–3 staff · 30–80 visits/day
Cost driverRent + staff; contribution margin 60–80%
Payer mixCash/UPI dominant; some corporate panels
1 · Business model
Revenue lineHow it worksMargin reality
ConsultationsFee-for-service per visit; most clinics honor an informal free re-visit window (5–7 days) for the same complaintCore revenue; capped by doctor hours × consult speed (5–10 min average)
ProceduresDressings, injections, nebulization, minor derm/dental proceduresHigh margin, low volume; often under-priced out of habit
In-house dispensingGP clinics in smaller towns dispense medicines directly ("doctor sahab's medicine")Meaningful margin but informal inventory; regulatory grey when no pharmacist
Panels & campsCorporate/TPA empanelment at discounted rates; school & society health camps as acquisitionLow rate, slow payment, but fills off-peak hours and feeds new families

The economic truth: the doctor is the product, the capacity, and the brand. Revenue grows only via fee increases, faster consults (quality risk), or added hours (burnout). Everything the business does exists to protect and fill doctor-hours.

4 · Patient journey
Discovery

Word-of-mouth first; Google Maps reviews and Practo/Justdial increasingly decisive for new families

Accessfriction

Walk in and take a token, or phone the receptionist. No reliable time estimate exists; patients budget half a day

The waitfriction

30–120 minutes in a crowded room. The wait is the #1 stated reason patients switch doctors — ahead of fees or outcomes

Consultation

5–10 minutes. History from memory (no prior record), exam, handwritten Rx. Instructions verbal, half-forgotten by the parking lot

Fulfillment

Pharmacy next door (or in-clinic dispensing); lab across the road if tests written. The clinic learns nothing about whether either happened

Follow-upleakage

Return only if symptoms persist or memory serves. Chronic patients silently lapse; the clinic has no way to notice

6 · Operational pain points
  • Queue chaos: unpredictable arrival + no time transparency = angry rooms, receptionist abuse, doctor pressure to rush
  • No longitudinal record: every visit restarts from memory; chronic care quality is capped by recall
  • Follow-up leakage: the largest silent revenue and outcome loss in the business — nobody measures it
  • Doctor dependency: illness or travel = zero revenue; no locum mechanism exists
  • Reception SPOF: one resignation and the clinic forgets its own operations
  • Panel receivables: months-late TPA payments, tracked in a notebook
7 · Software used today
CategoryWhat's actually in use
Practice mgmtPracto Ray, Eka Care, HealthPlix, DocOn, Bajaj Finserv Health — usually only the appointment + Rx-print slices
CommunicationWhatsApp — the true operating system: bookings, reports, reminders, payments
PaymentsUPI QR on the desk; PhonePe/Paytm statements as the de-facto ledger
EMRRare in earnest. Typing during a 6-minute consult is unrealistic, so adoption collapses after the trial month
8 · Biggest opportunities
  • Time transparency: live queue position + honest ETA converts the #1 pain into the #1 loyalty lever
  • Zero-typing records: capture built for a 6-minute consult (voice, templates, assistant-entered) — the only EMR that can survive here
  • Recall engine: systematic follow-up and chronic-care reminders recover the silent leakage — measurable, attributable revenue
  • ABHA-linked history: a clinic that can pull a patient's prior records becomes clinically better overnight — a differentiator small clinics could never build alone
  • Panel automation: claim paperwork and receivable tracking done by software, not the receptionist's evenings
9 · Future trends
  • Free-EMR land grab: HealthPlix/Eka giving software away to own the Rx data layer — clinics are being platformed whether they notice or not
  • ABDM incentives: digitization incentives + registry presence starting to matter for empanelment
  • Patient expectation shift: post-COVID normalization of online booking & teleconsult; younger families simply won't queue blind
  • Doctor as creator: Instagram/YouTube personal brands driving direct acquisition, bypassing aggregators
Challenge the workflow

The waiting room is not a feature of care — it is unpriced inventory buffering the doctor's utilization. The entire walk-in model exists because the clinic cannot predict consult durations or arrivals, so it makes patients absorb the variance with their time. Technology should not "digitize the token" — it should collapse the buffer: predicted per-token times, arrive-when-called, and honest overrun communication.

The free re-visit window is an untracked business rule. Every clinic runs one; none can say what it costs or earns. It should be an explicit, measured policy — it is the clinic's only retention product.

03 · Organization Archetype

Multi-specialty Clinic / Polyclinic

Several specialties under one roof, usually with an in-house lab, basic imaging and a pharmacy counter. The step between the solo clinic and the hospital — and the archetype where internal cross-referral becomes the business.

Revenue engineConsult fees + in-house lab & pharmacy margin
Capacity unitConsultant slots × room availability
Typical scale5–25 consultants (mostly visiting) · 10–40 staff · 100–400 visits/day
Cost driverConsultant fee-share + facility + staff
Payer mixCash + corporate panels + OPD insurance emerging
1 · Business model
Revenue lineHow it worksMargin reality
ConsultationsVisiting consultants on 60–80% fee share; a few salaried juniors for continuityThin for the center — consults are the feeder, not the profit
In-house diagnosticsLab + X-ray/USG capture tests written in-house; same-day loop back to the doctorThe margin engine — 40–60% contribution when volumes hold
Pharmacy counterFills in-house prescriptions before the patient reaches the exitSecond margin engine; capture rate is the metric nobody measures
Procedures / minor OTDaycare-adjacent minor procedures, dressings, endoscopy in larger setupsGood margin, scheduling-sensitive
Panels & corporatesCompany empanelments, pre-employment checkups, OPD insurance tie-insVolume at discount; paperwork-heavy, slow receivables

The real business: buy footfall with consults, monetize it with diagnostics and pharmacy. Success is measured (or should be) by internal capture rate — what fraction of prescribed tests and medicines stay in the building.

21:30+Cash close per counter (consult / lab / pharmacy separately), then a manual merge; consultant-share ledger updated in Excel

4 · Patient journey
Booking

Phone or walk-in dominant; aggregators (Practo) for new patients; direct WhatsApp for repeats

Registration & waitfriction

New UHID per center (patient's fourth this year); consult wait compounded by consultant's late arrival

Consultation

10–15 min; tests and medicines prescribed; "get these done and come back" — same-day loop if lab is quick

Diagnostics decisionleakage

Patient weighs in-house price vs the lab their neighbor recommended; 30–50% walk out with the prescription

Review & pharmacy

Report review same evening or next visit; pharmacy counter capture depends on queue length at that exact moment

Continuitygap

Each specialist keeps their own paper file; the patient with diabetes + cardiac + ortho is three unconnected records in one building

6 · Operational pain points
  • Consultant no-shows / delays: one late consultant torches a whole evening's bookings and the center's reputation, not the consultant's
  • Payout disputes: monthly reconciliation friction is the top reason consultants quit centers
  • Referral leakage: the margin engines starve while footfall is high — and it isn't even measured
  • Fragmented patient view: no single record across specialties in the same building
  • Discount leakage: unaudited front-desk discretion
  • Panel receivables: 60–120 day cycles, disputes over disallowed items
7 · Software used today
CategoryWhat's actually in use
Clinic HIMSInsta by Practo, SoftClinic, MocDoc, KareXpert lite tiers — usually billing + appointments modules only
LabStandalone LIS (CrelioHealth-class) or the HIMS lab module, rarely integrated with billing properly
PharmacyMarg ERP or GoFrugal POS — an island
AccountsTally, fed manually from the other three
The real systemExcel + WhatsApp — payouts, consultant schedules, panel tracking all live here
8 · Biggest opportunities
  • One patient, one record, all specialties: the multi-specialty promise, actually delivered — instant clinical differentiation
  • Transparent consultant settlements: per-visit attribution both sides can see kills the #1 relationship killer
  • Capture-rate visibility: measure and manage internal lab/pharmacy conversion — direct margin impact
  • Live schedule truth: consultant delay → automatic patient notification → the center stops eating the reputational cost
  • NHCX-era claims desk: one pipeline instead of seven TPA portals
9 · Future trends
  • Chain consolidation: organized clinic chains and insurer-owned OPD networks expanding into tier-2
  • OPD insurance: outpatient covers growing from near-zero — will industrialize panel workflows
  • Daycare pull: procedures migrating out of hospitals toward clinic + daycare formats (payer preference)
  • Franchise polyclinics: brand + SOP + software as a bundle sold to doctor-entrepreneurs
Challenge the workflow

"Come back with the reports" is a design failure, not a care model. The same-building loop (consult → lab → review) is the polyclinic's core promise, yet it runs on the patient physically ferrying paper between rooms. The loop should be an orchestrated same-day episode with the review slot reserved at order time.

Consultant payout opacity is a solvable trust problem being treated as an accounting chore. Attribution should be automatic at billing time, visible to the consultant continuously — reconciliation as a concept should disappear.

04 · Organization Archetype

Hospital

The 50–300 bed private hospital — India's secondary-care workhorse: nursing homes grown up, single-owner or small-group, mixed specialty with OT, ICU, emergency, in-house lab/imaging/pharmacy. (Corporate chains are a variant, not the norm.)

Revenue engineIPD packages — room, OT, consumables, pharmacy, investigations
Capacity unitBed-days & OT-hours; ARPOB × occupancy
Typical scale50–300 beds · 150–800 staff · occupancy 60–75%
Cost driverPeople — 55–70% of operating cost
Payer mixCash + retail insurance + PM-JAY + corporate/TPA
1 · Business model
Revenue lineHow it worksMargin reality
IPD (the core)Admissions from own OPD, emergency, and — critically — consultants who choose to admit here. Billed as room rent + surgeon fee + OT + consumables + pharmacy + investigations, or as payer-defined packagesPharmacy & investigations inside IPD carry the margin; room rent anchors the payer's rate card (everything scales off room category)
OPDConsultant chambers; deliberately cheapA feeder — measured by OPD→IPD conversion, not profit
Emergency24×7 casualty; medico-legal cases includedLoss-leader that feeds admissions and defines community trust
Diagnostics & pharmacyIn-house lab, imaging, 24×7 pharmacy serving IPD + walk-inHighest-margin departments in the building
PM-JAY / govt schemesFixed package rates per procedureOften below private cost — a volume & utilization play requiring ruthless case-mix and cost discipline

Two structural dependencies define the business: visiting consultants decide where patients get admitted (the hospital competes for doctors as much as for patients), and payer mix decides cash flow — every insurance/PM-JAY admission converts revenue into a receivable that must be defended paper by paper.

2 · Daily operations — a 24×7 machine with a daily heartbeat

20:00–07:00Skeleton night crew: duty doctors + night nursing; emergency keeps admitting; night is when thin staffing meets highest clinical risk

4 · Patient journey (planned admission — the revenue spine)
Decision

OPD consult or referral ends in "we should operate" — counseling desk quotes package by room category; insurance eligibility checked

Pre-admissionfriction

Pre-auth filed; approval may land the night before or the morning of — patients arrive not knowing if they're covered

Admission day

Registration → deposit (cash) or approval letter (insurance) → bed allotment → pre-op workup; family navigates 4 counters

Treatment

Surgery/procedure → ICU/ward → daily consultant rounds; family's information diet: corridor conversations and guesswork

Dischargethe notorious wait

"You can go home today" at 9 AM becomes an exit at 6 PM: summary → sign-off → returns → bill → TPA final approval. The worst-remembered part of the entire stay

Post-dischargecliff

A follow-up date on paper. No systematic contact until (or unless) the patient returns — readmission risk unmanaged, loyalty unearned

6 · Operational pain points
  • Discharge TAT: 4–8 hours of coordination failure — blocks beds (real revenue) and defines the family's lasting memory
  • Claims & receivables: denials, deductions, 30–90+ day cycles; PM-JAY reimbursement delays strain working capital
  • Consultant loyalty: admitters can move their patients to the hospital next door; relationship management is existential and entirely informal
  • Nursing attrition: constant churn = constant retraining = protocol drift and documentation gaps
  • Billing surprises: estimate vs final bill gap is the #1 trust destroyer and grievance source
  • OT utilization: first-case delays and turnover gaps waste the most expensive hours in the building
  • Paper ICU/ward charts: transcription errors, illegibility, zero analyzability; MRD retrieval takes days

8 · Biggest opportunities
  • Discharge as an orchestrated pipeline: parallel-track summary, returns, billing and payer approval with a visible countdown — hours recovered per bed per stay
  • Claims automation on NHCX rails: standardized submission, deduction analytics, receivable ageing visibility — direct working-capital impact
  • Live bed & OT truth: a real command view replacing whiteboard divergence; admission promises the front desk can actually keep
  • Consultant experience: mobile rounds view, one-tap discharge sign-off, transparent earnings — the retention tool for the hospital's true customers
  • Family communication: proactive status updates and honest running-bill visibility — kills the two biggest grievance classes at once
  • Post-discharge programs: structured follow-up converts episodic surgery into continuity (and defensible outcomes data)
9 · Future trends
  • Cashless-everything: insurance share of IPD climbing steadily; "cashless everywhere" initiatives + NHCX will make claims competence a core operating muscle, not a desk
  • PM-JAY volume play: tier-2/3 hospitals building whole service lines around scheme packages at scale
  • Daycare carve-out: payers pushing cataract/chemo/endoscopy-class procedures out of inpatient settings — hospitals must respond or lose the volume
  • Hospital-at-home: step-down and chronic IPD substitution pilots by insurers and chains
  • Staffing crunch deepens: global nurse demand (Gulf/UK/EU recruitment) keeps attrition structural — process resilience must come from systems, not tenure
Challenge the workflow

Discharge is treated as paperwork that begins when care ends. It should be a countdown that starts at admission. Every element of the 6-hour wait — summary, returns, bill, payer approval — is predictable on day one. The industry norm of sequential processing is a choice, not a constraint.

The TPA desk is a human API gateway. Skilled staff spend their days re-typing clinical and billing data into portals. NHCX makes this translation layer obsolete in principle; hospitals that restructure the revenue-cycle team around exceptions (denials, disputes) instead of data entry will run at a different cost base.

"The family will ask at the nursing station" is an information system. A deliberately bad one. Proactive status and cost communication is cheaper than the grievances, escalations and social-media fallout it prevents.

05 · Organization Archetype

Diagnostic Center

Pathology labs and radiology centers — from a single collection-center-plus-lab to hub-and-spoke networks. A logistics-and-quality business wearing a healthcare coat: samples in, trustworthy reports out, on time, every time.

Revenue engineB2C tests & packages + B2B lab-to-lab volume
Capacity unitAnalyzer throughput · radiologist reads · phlebo routes
Typical scale1 processing hub + 3–30 collection points · 200–2,000 samples/day
Cost driverReagents + machines (AMC/lease) + field staff
Payer mixCash/UPI dominant; corporate wellness contracts; scheme rates for empaneled
1 · Business model
Revenue lineHow it worksMargin reality
B2C prescribed testsWalk-in + home collection (60%+ of metro volume now); driven by doctor prescriptionsHealthy gross margin, but customer acquisition rides on referring doctors — see the commission problem below
Wellness packagesPre-designed panels (full body, diabetes, thyroid) sold direct to consumers — no prescription neededThe profit and growth engine: batched processing, predictable demand, marketing-driven — margin the referral economy can't tax
B2B (lab-to-lab)Small labs, clinics and hospitals outsource specialized tests to the hub at trade rates; monthly credit accountsThin per-test but fills machine capacity; TAT reliability is the entire relationship
RadiologyX-ray/USG/CT/MRI per scan; capex or lease-heavy; radiologist read is the bottleneckHigh ticket; utilization-sensitive; teleradiology increasingly rents out the bottleneck
Corporate & campsPre-employment, annual checkup contracts, on-site campsVolume at discount; seasonal; feeds the B2C funnel

The uncomfortable center of B2C economics: referral incentives to prescribing doctors — pervasive, legally grey, ethically corrosive, and margin-destroying. National players compete on brand + price to escape it; everyone else quietly pays. Any honest business model of this sector must name it.

6 · Operational pain points
  • TAT breaches: one machine down or one batch delayed → B2B clients defect; trust is rebuilt in months, lost in a day
  • Sample rejection & re-collection: costs double logistics + patient fury; usually a phlebotomy quality issue
  • Commission economics: 20–40% of a B2C test's price can be referral payout — a margin hemorrhage and an ethics trap
  • Price war: venture-funded players selling below cost reset consumer price anchors permanently
  • NABL burden: quality documentation consumes senior staff time; audits are dreaded events, not byproducts of operations
  • Radiologist scarcity: scan today, report in 3 days — the machine was never the bottleneck
  • B2B receivables: credit accounts drift; disputes over rate cards from three revisions ago

8 · Biggest opportunities
  • Longitudinal results as a product: trend lines across years (ABHA-linked) turn a PDF factory into a health-data relationship — the single biggest untapped asset in the sector
  • Report comprehension: plain-language explanation + flag triage + "show this to your doctor" routing — closes the abandonment gap and differentiates on trust
  • Order-to-report visibility: live status for patients and B2B clients kills the #1 inbound call driver
  • B2B portal: self-serve ordering, live TAT, transparent rate cards, automated statements — locks in client labs
  • Compliance as a byproduct: QC, critical-alert and audit trails generated by the workflow itself, not re-documented for NABL
9 · Future trends
  • Consolidation + aggregators: national chains and online players squeezing standalone labs into B2B suppliers or franchisees
  • Preventive & wellness growth: subscription checkups and direct-to-consumer panels growing faster than prescribed testing
  • AI reads: X-ray screening (TB triage already deployed at scale in India) and pathology assist — bottleneck relief, new QC questions
  • At-home everything: home collection normalized; home ECG/imaging vans emerging
  • Genomics mainstreaming: carrier panels and onco-genomics entering routine prescribing in metros
Challenge the workflow

The referral-commission economy is the sector's real operating system, and it is rotting the business. It taxes margins, criminalizes marketing, and steers patients by kickback rather than quality. The structural exit is making labs directly valuable to prescribers — instant results delivery into the doctor's workflow, trend context, critical alerts — so the referral is earned by service, not bought. A platform can make the clean path the convenient path.

A lab report's job is not to be delivered; it is to be understood and acted on. The industry optimizes TAT-to-PDF and declares victory. The unmeasured metric — did the result change care? — is where diagnostic value actually lives.

6 · Operational pain points
  • Inventory paradox: dead stock and stockouts simultaneously — 20,000+ SKUs, demand guessed store by store
  • E-pharmacy discounting: app players' funded discounts reset price expectations; store margins bleed to match
  • Pharmacist shortage / name-lending: regulatory exposure the whole sector pretends not to see
  • Expiry losses: slow movers expire on shelves; return negotiations recover only part
  • Rx ambiguity: illegible handwriting = dispensing risk carried personally by counter staff
  • B2B credit collection: clinic and nursing-home accounts drift 60–90 days
  • Zero adherence visibility: the chain's biggest revenue lever — chronic refills — is unmanaged

  • Formalize the chronic subscription: refill prediction + proactive outreach + auto-delivery turns the sector's silent core into managed recurring revenue — and measurably better adherence
  • Digital Rx rails: ABDM-era e-prescriptions kill handwriting risk, enable clean substitution logic, and create a legal dispensing trail — the compliance time bomb defused
  • Demand-driven inventory: store-level demand signals (including nearby prescriber patterns) against the dead-stock/stockout paradox
  • Counseling at scale: printed/WhatsApp dosage instructions per dispense — the pharmacist's clinical value delivered even under queue pressure
  • Clinic-pharmacy loop: prescription flows from nearby OPDs with patient consent — footfall earned by service, not proximity luck

  • Jan Aushadhi & generics push: government generic retail expanding; substitution normalization accelerates private-label economics

  • Store as care point: vaccination, BP/sugar checks, teleconsult kiosks — the pharmacy as primary-care front end (the global pattern arriving in India)
Challenge the workflow

Waiting for the chronic patient to remember their refill is the sector's largest self-inflicted wound. It harms adherence (a clinical outcome), revenue (the core stream), and loyalty (the switching moment is the stockout/forgotten-refill moment). Refill management isn't a CRM feature; it is the business model, unimplemented.

The paper prescription is a data black hole the entire chain of care pays for. The doctor loses fulfillment visibility, the pharmacy carries interpretation risk, the patient carries the paper. E-prescription rails exist now; the workflow persists out of habit, not necessity.

Challenge the workflow

Recurring care is run on tools built for one-off appointments. The dialysis roster is not "an appointment booked thrice weekly" — it is a standing clinical commitment with absence risk, backfill logic, and lifetime continuity. The industry bends appointment software around this and bleeds utilization at every bend.

The transfer protocol is a data handoff done by ambulance. A deteriorating patient arrives at the partner hospital with a verbal history and a photocopied file. The clinical record should arrive before the patient does.

Workflows this blueprint refuses to digitize as-is

The Session 1 brief demands we challenge workflows, not embalm them in software. These eight industry "normals" are design failures, and carrying any of them unchanged into Auriva would be a defect:

C1

The waiting room as capacity buffer

Patients' hours absorb the provider's scheduling variance — in clinics, labs, and day-care alike. Time transparency and called-when-ready flows must replace the buffer, not decorate it.

C2

Discharge as end-of-stay paperwork

Every component of the 4–8 hour hospital discharge is knowable at admission. It should run as a parallel pipeline with a countdown, initiated on day one.

C3

The human API gateway

TPA desks, panel executives and coordinators re-typing the same clinical/billing facts into payer portals. NHCX-era claims should be generated from the operational record, with humans on exceptions only.

C4

Follow-up as the patient's memory problem

Clinics lose chronic patients, pharmacies lose refills, hospitals lose post-op continuity — the same failure in four costumes. Recall is the provider's job; software must make it systematic.

C5

The paper prescription as data black hole

Doctor loses fulfillment visibility, lab and pharmacy inherit interpretation risk, the patient carries the only copy. The Rx is an order — it should flow as one.

C6

Reports delivered, not understood

Diagnostics optimizes TAT-to-PDF; comprehension and action are nobody's metric. Results must land with interpretation, trend context, and a route back to the prescriber.

C7

Referral by commission

The kickback economy steers patients by payout, not quality, and taxes every diagnostic rupee. The clean exit is making earned referral (service, speed, data-back-to-doctor) more convenient than the bought kind.

C8

Settlement by monthly dispute

Consultant fee-shares, B2B lab accounts, distributor credits — all reconciled after the fact in Excel, all generating friction. Attribution belongs at transaction time, visible to both sides continuously.

The network insight
One patient's single episode routinely touches four of these six organizations — clinic → lab → pharmacy → (sometimes) hospital or day-care — and today zero context travels with them. Every existing vendor sells software to one archetype and reproduces the silo. The ABDM rails (ABHA identity, consent-managed exchange, NHCX claims) now make the connective tissue nationally legal and technically standard. The deepest business opportunity identified in Session 1 is not any single org's efficiency: it is being the layer on which the patient's context, orders, results and money flow between organizations. That is what "Healthcare Operating System" must mean, and it is why Auriva's platform is organization-plural by design.
